Summary

What nine interviews add to the picture

The mechanism isn't "I decided to make the trip"; it's "I was already going to be there." Scheduling drop-off at first appointment is now a confirmed systemic problem, not an edge case.
  • 02 Platform and UX failures are breaking retention at multiple stages. Mason's account showed a missed appointment after results were ready; he contacted support twice. Sara experienced a chaotic, multi-check-in partner location onboarding in Nashville. These five failures are independent users with different profiles — they are not outliers.

Finding 01

Convenience is an ecosystem, not a feature — and scheduling drop-off at first appointment is systemic

The Listen Labs study established that 13 of 17 participants gated participation on a nearby, convenient location. These interviews reveal the mechanism more precisely: convenience isn't about willingness to make a trip to Goodlabs specifically, it's about whether Goodlabs can attach itself to a trip that's already happening.

"I used to have therapy near three blocks away from Good Labs. And I would just — so it felt like I could just kind of do that. I don't know how much I'll continue to make this the trek."

Kwan — SF Bay Area donor

Kwan's donation history maps almost directly to proximity to an anchor destination. When the therapy moved, the Goodlabs trip became its own errand. Mason's case shows what happens when the anchor disappears entirely — he would use Goodlabs regularly "if it were accessible," which means the demand is there; the supply infrastructure isn't. Victor adds a third dimension: the month-long lead time for appointment slots caused him to forget the appointment was scheduled and miss it. He had to rebook — another month out. The two-month delay between deciding to donate and actually doing it is a conversion window that closes for a lot of people.

Thomas is the counterpoint: he had to reschedule multiple times due to work and found the process easy and judgment-free. But Thomas's primary motivation was altruism, not convenience — he had a personal reason to persist through friction. For health-curiosity-motivated donors who haven't yet felt the value of the product, a month-long wait followed by a missed appointment is a dropout event, not a rescheduling event.

Scheduling Friction — Confirmed at Scale

Victor: missed first appointment due to 1-month lead time; had to reschedule. Kwan: appointment frequency dropped when anchor trip moved. Mason: geographically locked out after relocation. Aiman: concerns about scheduling and time commitment cited as barrier to first donation. This is now N=4+ independent data points confirming that first-appointment scheduling friction is a systemic dropout driver, not an anecdotal one.

Finding 04

Good Labs is winning on price against Function Health — but losing on per-biomarker education

Three interviews explicitly compared Good Labs to Function Health or Superpower. All three made similar calculations: Good Labs is cheaper and more flexible; Function and Superpower have better in-platform biomarker education. Carolee was actively evaluating Function Health's $500/year subscription before discovering Good Labs through a Vitalant in-center sign. Victor uses all three services and positioned each precisely.

Dimension Good Labs advantage Function Health / Superpower advantage
Price Free with donation; a la carte ordering; donor interview-quoted drop from ~$1,500 to $200–300 for comparable panels Function: $500/yr subscription; Superpower: faster scheduling (2–3 days) but expensive add-ons
Flexibility A la carte test selection; immediate results as they come in (Function requires contacting support for partial results) Function: comprehensive fixed panel; less control over specific test selection
Results speed Results available immediately as they come in; no support contact required Function held partial Quest results behind customer support contact
Per-biomarker education Range indicators (red/green) are sufficient for casual users; less explanatory depth Function: per-biomarker educational content with cited sources, optimal vs. lab-normal ranges, mechanistic explanations (e.g., ferritin's role in neurochemical support even when technically in-range)
AI feature Embedded AI chat for results interpretation; preferred for data privacy vs. copy-pasting into ChatGPT; CSV export + MCP integration for power users Superpower: AI chat with "nice design" but "not meaningfully better than ChatGPT with personal labs"

"The price has dropped from about $1,500 to $200–300 for a comprehensive panel. That's what makes it so easy to recommend now."

Dillon — Seattle, Amazon engineer, comparing Good Labs to Function Health

Dillon is the most acute case: he describes the per-biomarker education gap as the one area where Function Health still has an edge, and bridges it himself by exporting his Good Labs CSV and feeding it into Claude AI projects he maintains for different health phases. This is both a product feedback signal (the gap is real) and a retention signal (his workaround is too sophisticated for most users to replicate — the gap is leaving capability on the table for the majority).

The competitive insight is that the price advantage is decisive for this cohort. None of them left Function Health or Superpower because of a bad experience — they chose Good Labs because $0 beats $500 when the core use case is covered. That means Good Labs doesn't need to match Function Health on features to win. But closing the per-biomarker education gap would convert the users who currently have a competing subscription they're keeping for that depth.

Finding 05

The AI feature is a meaningful differentiator for engaged users — and invisible to everyone else

Across nine interviews, the AI feature reception split almost perfectly by user engagement level. Three users engaged deeply; three were aware but hadn't tried it; three had no interaction with it.

Deep engagement: Dillon uses Good Labs CSV exports with Claude AI + MCP integration for near-weekly differential diagnosis research — he maintains separate Claude projects for different health phases (pre- and post-diagnosis). He describes the MCP integration as something he requested, and that it shipped within 1–2 months of his suggestion. Aiman used both the Good Labs AI and Gemini (with browser control) to construct his test cart and build an action plan from results. Both users are software engineers who treat their health data as a dataset to query.

Valued in-platform: Sara specifically preferred having the AI embedded in the platform over copy-pasting into ChatGPT, for two reasons: it interprets results in the context of each other (not just one marker in isolation), and it feels more private for sensitive health data. She described it as the major differentiator over her annual GP bloodwork — the AI explains what two low values mean together, and gives actionable next steps.

"It felt more secure. I wasn't putting my health data into some random chat window. And it knew what the other results were — it could tell me how two things related to each other."

Sara — Nashville, Wellness Maintainer

Disengaged: Thomas doesn't use AI for health data — he prefers to share results with his father and brother-in-law (both doctors) and ask "how do we make it better?" He found the in-app range indicators (red/green) clear and sufficient. Victor hadn't tried the AI feature at all. Carolee made no mention of it.

The AI feature is currently a power-user retention tool. The opportunity is in the middle — users like Sara who find it valuable for interpretation but might not have discovered it without using it in context. The feature needs a stronger in-product discovery moment to convert casual users into engaged ones.

Finding 06

There's a distinct Access Seeker sub-profile in tech markets: the coverage-gap and the time-constrained

The existing Access Seeker framing covers the chronically uninsured. These interviews surface two variations concentrated in tech markets.

The coverage-gap donor (Kwan): Previously had exceptional employer-sponsored insurance and now has a gap. The draw isn't crisis care — it's the loss of a perk she took for granted. She described it precisely: "I used to be spoiled by really good health insurance. I don't get to indulge straight curiosities as much." Goodlabs fills that curiosity gap without requiring a doctor's visit or out-of-pocket payment. This profile is likely concentrated in SF, Seattle, and NYC where tech-worker insurance is unusually good and its loss is unusually felt.

The time-constrained direct-pay user (Aiman): He has insurance — his annual wellness visit is covered. But doctors won't order tests without medical justification, scheduling takes ~1 month, and the interface (individual PDFs, hard to parse) is frustrating. He wants to make a lifestyle change and re-test in 3 months to see the impact. Insurance can't accommodate that cadence. Good Labs can.

The messaging implication differs for each. "Free bloodwork, no insurance needed" speaks to Mason — someone for whom cost is the barrier. The coverage-gap donor needs something closer to "get the testing you're used to, without the plan you used to have." The time-constrained user responds to "on your timeline, not your doctor's" — it's about speed and control, not cost.

Finding 07

Symptom-based panels are a product gap with apparent willingness to pay

Four of nine donors, unprompted, described wanting some version of a symptoms-first entry point into test selection or results interpretation.

"If there was one that was like 'bad sleep' — things that generally impact bad sleep — I'd be like, okay, I don't want to bother to research what the main things are. But if there's one that's panels related to what life might feel like."

Kwan

Kwan's frustration was specific: she kept a notes-app list of tests she'd read about, then had to control-find each one while navigating different spellings, abbreviations, and parenthetical formats. Mason described "I feel tired" as a lower-barrier entry point. Sara appreciated the pre-grouped Nashville panels organized by health concern and found the AI feature most useful for explaining correlations between results she didn't expect to be related. Emily wanted journal prompts correlating how she feels with what her labs show — and said explicitly she would pay for this feature.

These are four different versions of the same gap: the interface between how people experience symptoms and how tests are categorized. It surfaces at test selection (Kwan, Mason) and at results interpretation (Emily, Sara). Both ends of the test journey have the same mismatch.

Finding 08

Partner location quality at launch is a first-impression problem that marketing cannot solve

Two interviews — Sara in Nashville and Carolee in SF — flagged meaningfully different partner-location issues. Together they illustrate that the physical donation experience is Good Labs' largest uncontrolled brand variable.

Sara's Nashville experience was the more acute case. The partner location was newly launched and experiencing a surge in donation volume. Multiple redundant check-ins (Good Labs, front desk, donation center physical check-in). Same information entered across all three systems. Many staff cycling in and out without clear handoffs. Medical terminology used without explanation ("double red donation"). Difficulty finding a vein required switching from machine to manual process.

"It was just a lot of people and not that much organization or communication about what was happening. They also sometimes would use terminology that I didn't quite understand."

Sara — Nashville, first-time donor

Sara understood the context and was forgiving. But she is a Wellness Maintainer who came in via friend referral with high intent — exactly the profile Good Labs wants to retain. A first-time donor with lower intent, less patience for a chaotic experience, or higher needle anxiety would not schedule a second appointment.

Carolee's issue was a digital ops problem at an established location: a sync disconnect between the Good Labs booking system and Vitalant's system, combined with iron-deferral-related account bugs that persisted. The on-site Good Labs rep acknowledged the account was "messed up." She also noted that some Vitalant staff prefer donors to book directly through Vitalant, creating a subtle misalignment between partner-staff incentives and Good Labs' platform engagement goals.

The implication is that quality control at partner-location launch — specifically the check-in experience, staff communication protocol, and booking system reliability — is as critical to retention as the digital platform itself. The pre-donation emails are excellent (multiple donors praised them independently). The gap is between what donors expect after those emails and what they encounter at the physical location.

Finding 09

Seattle expansion has strong existing demand; Reno is a named gap with specific infrastructure

Seattle is Good Labs' most concrete near-term expansion story in this dataset. Good Labs is launching blood draw centers in the Pacific Northwest in the first week of August, with a planned meetup for approximately 1,000 existing local users. Dillon is a power user in Seattle who has not yet donated because no location existed nearby; he's waiting until after surgery in September. Aiman is a direct-pay customer in Seattle who has never donated; the August launch is the stated trigger for him to try donation. Victor referred a friend who couldn't sign up because Good Labs isn't in Houston — market coverage is actively capping word-of-mouth conversion.

Expansion Signal — Reno, NV

Mason named Quest Diagnostics centers in Reno specifically as existing infrastructure not yet partnered with Goodlabs. He is an eligible, motivated, regular donor who is currently unable to use the service. This is the most specific and actionable expansion signal from any research to date: a named market, a named partner candidate, and a confirmed willing user.

Finding 10

TPE: body stakes matter more than price, and trust requires published research in accessible formats

Five of nine donors commented on therapeutic plasma exchange across the interviews. No one had heard of it beforehand. The barrier is consistent: something going back into the body is a categorically different risk calculation than something leaving it.

Kwan drew the clearest distinction: "Blood donation is a known thing. Having it actually be doing something to a thing and putting it back in my body feels like very different stakes." She would only consider it if she independently decided she wanted the treatment first — Good Labs' role would be as a lower-cost provider of something she'd already researched and chosen. Carolee, perimenopausal, was skeptical about anything that could disrupt hormones; she would not respond to a social media ad for TPE and would need peer recommendation or endorsement from a vetted doctor.

Emily was the most open — she's accustomed to medical procedures for autoimmune conditions. Her trust-building requirement was specific: "trustworthy, published research presented in accessible formats — video snippets, something digestible." She had observed someone receiving what appeared to be TPE at the clinic during her own donation and hadn't asked about it. That passive exposure channel is worth noting: it didn't trigger anxiety for Emily, but it might for a first-time donor without her context.

Aiman was intrigued but wanted to research potential downsides before considering it — he prefers actual biomarker data (like Good Labs displays) over a summary claim like "decreased biological age." Skepticism toward longevity marketing claims was consistent, but he said he would pay ~$10K if scientific evidence clearly supported a 5-year biological age reduction — the issue is credibility of the claim, not cost.

Implications

What nine interviews add to the strategy

Product
Fix appointment status accuracy and account linkage before building new features

Appointment showing "missed" when results are ready. Seven months of inaccessible results from a December 2024 donation. Vitalant–Good Labs booking sync disconnects. These are trust-destroying moments at the most sensitive part of the user journey. The benchmark being referenced organically is Guava's auto-EHR sync — the path there doesn't start with full integration, it starts with accurate status states and reliable account linking.

Product
Close the per-biomarker education gap to reduce the Function Health overlap audience

Good Labs wins on price; Function Health wins on depth. Three users are holding Function or Superpower subscriptions alongside Good Labs specifically for per-biomarker educational content (cited sources, optimal vs. lab-normal ranges, mechanistic explanations). Dillon is bridging the gap himself with Claude AI + CSV exports. The feature gap is real, fillable, and is currently keeping a subset of users in a competing subscription rather than being fully consolidated on Good Labs.

Product
Symptom-based panels and results correlation have apparent willingness to pay across multiple user types

"Bad sleep," "fatigue," "stress" as entry points to test selection — paired with journal prompts correlating how the user feels with what labs show over time. Four users surfaced some version of this independently. It appears at test selection and results interpretation. Emily said she would pay for it. Sara found the pre-grouped panels immediately appealing when a friend described them. The feature bridges the mismatch between how people experience symptoms and how tests are categorized.

Retention
Reduce first-appointment drop-off — the conversion window closes before the product has been experienced

Victor missed his first appointment after a month-long lead time and waited another month to donate. The month between scheduling and appointment is a conversion window during which interest decays. Interventions: shorter lead times, a calendar reminder sequence in the weeks before, and a "you're near a Goodlabs center" proximity nudge for donors who are already downtown for another appointment (the anchor-trip mechanism Kwan and Thomas both demonstrated).

Retention
Partner-location launch quality requires an ops playbook, not just a marketing playbook

Sara's chaotic Nashville experience was the result of a newly-partnered location experiencing a surge in volume without a streamlined check-in protocol. First-time donors with lower intent or higher needle anxiety would not return. The pre-donation communication quality is excellent (multiple donors praised it independently) — the gap is between what donors expect after those emails and what they encounter at the physical location. A partner-location onboarding playbook with simplified check-in, staff communication protocols, and terminology guidance is a retention tool, not just an ops nice-to-have.

Messaging
Altruism can be a conversation-starter if the referrer has a personal story — write referral content for both profiles

Thomas ("I saved three lives today") and Emily ("free lab work, no doctor visit") represent the two ends of the referral spectrum. Thomas's altruism-first hook generates curiosity that naturally migrates to the health testing close — but only because his sister needed blood transfusions. Without a personal tie, altruism doesn't open that door. Give high-intent referrers the language for their specific profile: a testing-first frame for most, and an impact-storytelling frame for those with personal altruistic motivation. Thomas already mentioned GL to ~20 people at a single gathering.

Messaging
The Access Seeker needs two different frames depending on whether the barrier is cost or speed

"Free bloodwork, no insurance needed" speaks to Mason — cost is the barrier. "Get the testing you're used to, without the plan you used to have" speaks to Kwan — she had good access and lost it temporarily. "On your timeline, not your doctor's" speaks to Aiman — he has insurance but it's too slow and limiting for his monitoring cadence. These are distinct sub-profiles in the same bucket that require different messaging handles.

Expansion
Seattle August launch has ~1,000 existing users and two confirmed interview participants who will donate upon launch

Dillon and Aiman are both Seattle-area users who have not yet donated — not because of low intent, but because the infrastructure didn't exist. Both expressed clear intent to donate when the August launch opens. The meetup for existing local users is well-timed: the word-of-mouth audience in Seattle is already present and health-optimization-oriented (the "Huberman listener" audience that Victor named as a natural fit). Victor's failed referral to Houston also confirms that market availability is actively capping word-of-mouth conversion.

Study limitations: N=9, all existing Goodlabs donors or users — findings reflect post-conversion experience, not pre-conversion hesitation. Sample skews toward SF Bay Area, Seattle, and tech-adjacent profiles; three interviews are software engineers. Referral via friend accounts for most; one Reddit-based discovery, one Vitalant in-center discovery. Rural Southern, chronically-uninsured, and Spanish-speaking profiles remain unrepresented. Two participants (Aiman, Dillon) have not yet donated — their donation behavior is anticipated, not observed. Treat as directional hypotheses for broader testing, not generalizable conclusions.
